Poof

Privacy Policy

Effective October 4, 2026

This document explains exactly what data Poof accesses on your device, what it does with that data, and what (if anything) leaves your device. Poof is built on three commitments:

  • No ads. Ever.
  • No tracking by us. No analytics, no crash reporting, no advertising ID and no device identifiers of our own. Two Google libraries in Poof send Google their own diagnostic data: ML Kit, which reads barcodes, text and faces on your phone and never sends your pictures or what it reads, and Google Play's billing library; see Google ML Kit and Play Billing.
  • No accounts. You don't sign in. There is no Poof server holding your data because there is no Poof server.

If at any point this policy contradicts what the app actually does, the app is wrong; please contact us so we can fix it.

The short version

Poof is a utility toolkit. Most tools work entirely on your device and make no network request of their own. Six tools do, and only when you actively use them: Speed Test, Currency Converter, DNS Lookup, URL Unshortener, What's My IP, and Recipes. Four of those contact a fixed service chosen by us. Two, URL Unshortener and Recipes, contact a web address you give them and nothing else. Each is described in full under "What leaves your device" below. No request these tools make to any third party contains your contacts, files, location, notifications, or any user-identifying information. Separately, the tools that read barcodes, text or faces use Google's ML Kit. It works on your phone and never sends your pictures or what it reads, but it does send Google usage metrics, such as your phone model, Poof's version, an identifier for this installation and how long a scan took, and Google Play's billing library sends Google its own diagnostic logs; see Google ML Kit and Play Billing. And if you turn on Google backup in Poof's settings, Android can include Poof's data, including your notification history, saved locations and birthday entries, in its own end-to-end encrypted device backup, and in a direct transfer when you set up a new phone from this one; see Google backup.

When a tool needs access to something on your device (your camera, microphone, location, contacts, notifications, or installed apps, and on Android 8 and 9 only, your shared Pictures and Downloads folders when you save a file there), Android prompts you to grant permission, and you can revoke it at any time from Android Settings. Poof never accesses any of these without an explicit user action.

What we access, and where it goes

1. Location

Tools
Prayer Times, Sun & Moon (sunrise/sunset), Qibla, GPS Logger, Altimeter, Parking Spot Saver.
Accessed
Your device's GPS coordinates (latitude/longitude) via Android's standard Location Manager. Both Approximate and Precise location may be requested; coarse coordinates are used as fallback. GPS Logger additionally records a stream of coordinates over time while you have a recording session active, and may use the Background location permission so the session can continue while the screen is off (you explicitly grant this via Android Settings; the recording works without it while the screen is on and the foreground notification is visible).
Used for
Calculating prayer times, sunrise/sunset, and Qibla bearing locally on the device. Prayer Times stores your last-known coordinates in app-private storage so it can compute prayer times offline. If you pick a city by hand, it also keeps up to two of the cities you picked, so you can switch between them in one tap from its main screen; each is saved as the city's name, country and time zone with the centre coordinates from Poof's built-in city list, never your own position, alongside your other Prayer Times settings in app-private storage. GPS Logger stores the recorded track (a list of points with timestamps and optional altitude/accuracy/speed) in a local database on your device; you can rename, export, or delete tracks at any time. Parking Spot Saver stores the single spot you save, plus any note or photo you attach to it, in parking-spots.db, so it can point you back to the car later.
Goes to
Nowhere. Poof never transmits your coordinates. There is no Poof server. GPS tracks exported as GPX or KML are saved to a destination you pick via the system share sheet; nothing is uploaded by Poof. GPS tracks are never part of the cloud backup. On Android 12 and later they are carried only by a direct phone-to-phone transfer, and only if you turn on Google backup in Poof's settings. If you do turn it on, the last location Prayer Times and Sun & Moon remember, the cities Prayer Times keeps for switching, and your saved parking spot are included in your own end-to-end encrypted device backup; see Google backup.

Prayer Times, Sun & Moon, and Qibla accept manual city entry instead of GPS. GPS Logger requires GPS to record. Location permission can be revoked any time from Android Settings.

2. Camera

Tools

Accessed
Camera frames, in memory, while a camera screen in Poof is open, or the single photo you take. Document Scanner and Family Papers never see camera frames: Google's document scanner hands Poof only the finished pages.
Used for
QR & Barcode Scanner, Camera Inspector, Image OCR, the Authenticator and Loyalty Cards look for codes or text in what the camera sees with Google's ML Kit, on the phone, and ID Photo finds the face in the photo you take the same way. ML Kit never sends Google your camera frames, your photos or what it reads from them, but it does send Google usage metrics; see Google ML Kit and Play Billing. Document Scanner and Family Papers open Google's document scanner, which runs its own camera screen inside Google Play services and hands Poof only the finished pages. Magnifier and Live Color Picker only show the live image or read a colour from it, in memory, and Parking Spot Saver and Vehicle Log only take the photo you ask for; none of these four uses ML Kit.
Goes to
Nowhere we control. Live frames are analyzed in memory and never uploaded, by Poof or by ML Kit; only ML Kit's usage metrics go to Google. Parking Spot Saver, Vehicle Log and ID Photo keep a photo you take with them in Poof's app-private storage on your device; deleting the entry deletes the photo. Those photos are never part of the copy in your Google account; if you turn on Google backup, a direct phone-to-phone transfer on Android 12 and later carries them. Family Papers keeps its scans encrypted, and no backup or transfer ever carries them, as described under Family Papers below. Document Scanner holds the pages you scan in the app's cache only while you work on them: they are deleted when you remove a page or leave the tool, and any left behind when the app is closed mid-scan are deleted on a later visit to the tool. A Checklist, such as the papers for a medical claim, a move or a school transfer, is the exception: it keeps each paper's scans in Poof's app-private storage so you can finish the list another day, until you remove that paper, scan it again or clear the list. Saving the list as a PDF does not remove them. Those scans leave Poof only in a PDF you save or share: they are never part of a Google backup, and a phone-to-phone transfer never carries them. A list you keep for next time holds only its name, its papers' names and page limits and whether it saves one PDF or one per paper, never a scan, in scanner_lists.json; if you turn on Google backup, it is included in your own end-to-end encrypted device backup.
Stock count (Tally)
With Tally switched on, the QR & Barcode Scanner counts each code it reads, from the camera or from an image you pick, instead of opening it. The tally, which is each code, the name you give it, its count and when it was last counted, is kept in app-private storage under qr_tally so an interrupted count carries on, until you remove the code, clear the tally or uninstall Poof. Tally scans are not added to the scan history. Export CSV writes the codes, names and counts to the app's temporary cache and hands the file to Android's share sheet, to an app you pick; that cached copy is deleted the next time Poof starts. Load names reads a file you pick with Android's file picker and takes only its codes and names; the file itself is not kept. Poof never transmits the tally. If you turn on Google backup in Poof's settings, it is included in your own end-to-end encrypted device backup and in a direct phone-to-phone transfer; see Google backup.

12. Diary

Tool
Diary: private on-device journal with one entry per day, mood tracking, photos, calendar view, streaks, and daily reminders. Every entry is encrypted on this device with AES-256-GCM and a master key generated and held by Android's Keystore. The key requires user authentication (fingerprint, face, or screen-lock credential) before it can be used; once you authenticate, it stays usable for up to ten minutes so you can browse without re-prompting on every tap.
Accessed
The text, mood selection, and photos you choose to attach to each daily entry. Photos are added by picking from your gallery using Android's system Photo Picker (up to 5 per entry). The tool reads your device's wall clock and time zone so the daily reminder fires at the time you configure.
Used for
Storing each entry's content, mood, and photo-path list as ciphertext blobs in an on-device database. Only the date (the primary key) and the created / updated timestamps stay in the clear so the calendar can sort by recency without unlocking. Attached photos are downscaled to roughly 1600 px on the long edge, then encrypted with the same Keystore master key and written to the app's private files directory. The decrypted-in-memory data drives the in-app calendar, streak counter, mood chart, "On this day" memory card, and the optional daily reminder notification. The home-screen widget shows only date-derived information (entry-streak count, "written today" presence flag). it has no auth context and can't decrypt content or mood.
Goes to
Nowhere. Entries and photos live only on this device. There is no cloud copy and no automatic backup. If you uninstall Poof, use Android's "Clear data", or turn your screen lock off (None or Swipe), or a work or device admin resets your screen lock, the Keystore master key is wiped and the entries become unrecoverable by design, unless you exported an encrypted backup first (below). Changing your PIN, pattern or password, or adding a fingerprint or face, does not affect them. Sharing exports through Android's standard share sheet routes to whichever app you choose; Poof does not upload anything on its own.
Auto-lock
The diary re-locks the moment the whole app moves to the background. Re-entering the tool prompts for authentication again. The "Lock now" menu option locks manually without leaving. Authentication is handled entirely by Android's secure subsystem; no biometric data is exposed to Poof.
Independent from other tools
Diary uses a separate Keystore alias from Secure Vault, Encrypted Notes and Family Papers. Resetting one tool doesn't touch the others' keys, and each tool prompts for authentication separately the first time you enter it in a session. Turning the screen lock off, or a reset by a work or device admin, is the exception: Android then destroys all of these keys at once.
Plain-text exports you can create
The tool offers two unencrypted export formats via Android's system file picker (you choose the destination: local folder, Drive, email attachment, anywhere). These deliberately leave the tool's protection so you can use the data outside Poof:
  • Markdown: one text file containing every entry's date, mood, and content. Photos are not included; the markdown is plain text.
  • PDF: a fully rendered document containing every entry's text and attached photos, paginated. Used for printing.
Importing markdown back into the tool restores the text + mood entries; photo paths are dropped (they reference another device's filesDir and can't be migrated cross-install).
Optional encrypted backup
You can also export the whole diary into a single password-protected .poofdiary file. The format wraps a ZIP (manifest plus one plaintext JPEG per attachment) in AES-256-GCM, with the encryption key derived from your password using PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 over 250 000 iterations. The password is never stored anywhere, transmitted, or recoverable by Poof: if you forget it the backup is unreadable. You choose where the file is saved (local storage, your own cloud, etc.) using Android's system file picker. Restore reads the same format back and re-encrypts photos with the local Keystore key, dedupes by date so re-running the restore doesn't duplicate entries.

16. Secure Vault

Tool
Secure Vault: hide photos behind your phone's screen lock. Imported items are encrypted on-device and a small thumbnail is kept (also encrypted) so the grid can render without re-decrypting the full file each time.
Accessed
The images you pick from your gallery via Android's system Photo Picker. The original files in your gallery are not modified by default. If you turn on "Delete originals after import" in the vault's settings, the original is removed from your gallery once the encrypted copy is safely written; this is an explicit, off-by-default choice that uses Android's standard "delete these items?" system prompt to obtain your consent.
Used for
Storing each item as a ciphertext file under the app's private files directory, encrypted with AES-256-GCM. A random per-item data key encrypts the image bytes; the Keystore master key in turn encrypts the data key. The master key requires user authentication (fingerprint, face, or screen-lock credential) before it can be used; once you authenticate, it stays usable for up to ten minutes so you can browse the vault without re-prompting on every tap. A small encrypted thumbnail is also generated for the grid view. Each item's original file name, type and size, and when you added it, are kept unencrypted in the vault's database so the grid can sort and name them without unlocking every item. The full image is decrypted into memory only when you tap to view it; nothing plaintext is written back to disk.
Goes to
Nowhere on its own. Vault contents never leave your device. The tool makes no network requests. There is no cloud copy. If you uninstall Poof, use Android's "Clear data", or turn your screen lock off (None or Swipe), or a work or device admin resets your screen lock, the vault contents are unrecoverable by design unless you exported a password-protected backup first (see below). Changing your PIN, pattern or password, or adding a fingerprint or face, does not affect them. You can also choose to "Export to gallery" individual items or a multi-select batch, in which case the decrypted copy is written back to Pictures/Poof via Android's MediaStore. On Android 8 and 9, Poof first asks for storage access, which those versions need to save to the gallery.
Auto-lock
The vault re-locks the moment the whole app moves to the background (recents peek, switching to another app, screen off). Re-entering the tool always prompts for authentication. The lock icon in the top bar locks it manually without leaving the screen. Authentication is handled entirely by Android's secure subsystem; no biometric data is exposed to Poof, and only the master key's metadata is stored.
Optional UI prefs
A "Blur thumbnails" toggle blurs grid previews until you tap an item (purely visual, the underlying ciphertext is unchanged). A sort-order preference (newest first, oldest first, name A to Z or Z to A, size descending or ascending) and the "Delete originals after import" choice are stored in a small unencrypted preferences file. Speed Test → Cloudflare

When you run a speed test, the tool exchanges data with Cloudflare's public speed-test infrastructure:

  • https://speed.cloudflare.com/meta : returns your public IP, ISP name, and detected city
  • https://speed.cloudflare.com/__down : downloads test bytes for download-speed measurement
  • https://speed.cloudflare.com/__up : uploads random bytes for upload-speed measurement

Test results are saved to a local on-device database so you can see your history. Each saved result holds the speeds, latency and jitter, plus the public IP address, internet provider, and approximate city and country that Cloudflare reported for your connection, and the name of the Wi-Fi network you were on when Android makes it available to the app. Poof never sends your test results to us or to anyone else. If you turn on Google backup in Poof's settings, saved results, including those IP addresses, cities and network names, are included in your own end-to-end encrypted device backup; see Google backup. Cloudflare's privacy policy applies to the Cloudflare side: cloudflare.com/privacypolicy

Currency Converter → open.er-api.com

When you open the Currency Converter, it fetches current exchange rates from https://open.er-api.com/v6/latest/{currency}. The request contains only the base-currency code (e.g. USD). Rates are cached on your device so the converter works offline.

Nothing you type goes with that request. Conversions, and the sums Counter quote makes with an exchange counter's rate and fee, are worked out on the device. The converter remembers your favourite and recent currencies, the pair you used last, and, for each pair, the counter's rate and fee you entered, in app-private storage, so a regular transfer is ready next time; the amounts you type are not kept. If you turn on Google backup in Poof's settings, these are included in your own end-to-end encrypted device backup; see Google backup.

DNS Lookup → Cloudflare, Google

When you run a DNS lookup, the tool sends the hostname or IP you typed to one or more public DNS-over-HTTPS resolvers and shows you what they returned. The request contains only the hostname (or IP for reverse lookups) and the record type (A, AAAA, MX, etc.) you picked. The endpoints used are:

  • https://1.1.1.1/dns-query (Cloudflare)
  • https://dns.google/resolve (Google Public DNS)

In Compare mode the same request is sent in parallel to both so you can see how their answers differ. In Single mode only the resolver you chose is contacted. The tool can also resolve via the device's system DNS for A and AAAA records (your phone's normal resolver path, controlled by Android Settings or Private DNS), in which case the request goes wherever Android sends it. Each resolver's privacy policy applies to its side: Cloudflare, Google Public DNS.

Recipes → whichever recipe page you link to

When you paste a recipe link into Recipes, or share one to Poof from your browser, the tool fetches that page and looks for the structured recipe data (schema.org Recipe, published as JSON-LD) that most recipe sites already include for search engines. Like the URL Unshortener, and unlike the other tools here, it does not contact a fixed service: it contacts the address you gave it, and nothing else. There is no intermediary, no proxy, and no server of ours involved at any point.

The request is a plain GET from your device's normal IP, with a generic Poof user-agent string and no cookies, no identifiers, and nothing about you or your cookbook attached. The site can see your IP making the request, in the same way it would if you opened the page in a browser. The response is read up to a two megabyte limit and then discarded; only the recipe fields you approve on the review screen are kept, and only once you press Save.

If the page carries a photo and you leave the photo switch on, one further request is made to that same site for that image, which is then downscaled and stored on your device. Turning the switch off means no image request is made at all. No other address is ever contacted, no matter what the page contains.

URL Unshortener → whichever URL you paste

The URL Unshortener follows every redirect of a short URL you give it and shows the final destination plus a preview of that page's title and description. This is different from the tools above: it doesn't contact a fixed service. It contacts whatever URL you typed and whatever URL that one redirects to, all the way to the end of the chain (up to ten hops). Each request goes out from your device's normal IP, with a generic browser-style User-Agent and no cookies or identifiers.

By design, this means the destination server can see your IP making the request, in the same way it would if you tapped the link in any browser. That is the point of the tool: it visits the URL on your behalf so you can see where it goes before deciding to open it yourself. Nothing about the traced URL or the destination is stored after you leave the screen.

What's My IP → ipwho.is, icanhazip.com

When you open the What's My IP tool, it makes up to three requests, all in parallel:

  • https://ipwho.is/ : returns your public IP plus geolocation and network metadata (city, region, country, ISP, ASN, timezone). Whichever IP version your device's preferred stack chose comes back here.
  • https://ipv4.icanhazip.com : returns your IPv4 address as plain text, or fails silently if your network has no IPv4 path.
  • https://ipv6.icanhazip.com : returns your IPv6 address as plain text, or fails silently if your network has no IPv6 path.

Each service identifies you by the source IP of the request itself, the same way any website sees your IP. No request body, no API key, no account, no device identifier. The tool does not store the result; closing the screen discards it. icanhazip.com is operated by Cloudflare. The relevant privacy policies are: ipwho.is, Cloudflare.

Google ML Kit and Play Billing → Google

Poof uses Google's ML Kit to read QR codes, barcodes and text, to find a document's edges, to find faces and to separate a person from the background. The work happens on your phone. In Google's words, "processing of the input data (e.g. images, video, text) fully happens on-device, and ML Kit does not send that data and the resultant outputs to Google servers." Poof does not send them either.

ML Kit does send Google metrics about how it ran; Poof does not read or change them.   • https://firebaselogging.googleapis.com (Google)

Google says it uses them "to measure performance, debug, maintain and improve the APIs, and detect misuse or abuse", and that ML Kit does not transfer them to third parties. They go to Google, not to us. ML Kit may also contact Google from time to time for bug fixes, updated models and information about the machine-learning hardware in your phone. Google backup

Poof can take part in Android's own device backup, but only if you turn it on: Settings, then "Back up to your Google account". It is off until you do. The choice is per device: the toggle itself is never backed up or transferred, so turning it on here turns it on nowhere else. Poof itself never uploads your backup; Android does, as part of the device backup it already keeps for your other apps, held by your device's backup service (your own Google account on a Google-backed phone), in space that Google currently does not count against your Drive storage.

Poof adds your data to that backup only when Android reports that it is encrypted end to end with your screen lock, so that neither we nor Google can read it. That needs a screen lock on every Android version: without one, Poof's part of the backup holds nothing but the empty placeholder described below, and a note under the toggle in Settings says so. On Android 8 the toggle is not offered, and Poof's part of the backup is only that placeholder.

What is included: your lists, trackers, records and settings. Shopping Run, the text of your Recipes, Habits, Subscriptions, Vehicle Log, Medication Tracker, Qada Tracker, Zakat and Gold records, Settle Up, Gift Ledger, Receipt Book with your saved signature, Birthdays, Loyalty Cards, Countdowns, Notification History, your Prayer Times settings and recent cities, the signatures and stamps you save in PDF Sign, the lists you keep in Document Scanner (names and settings only, never scans), and the saved data of every other tool not named in the next paragraph, including the details you save in PDF Fill. What is never included in the copy in your Google account, whatever the toggle says: Secure Vault, Encrypted Notes, Diary, the Authenticator and Family Papers, because their keys live in this phone's hardware and a copy anywhere else would be unreadable; PDF Fill drafts; the scans in a Document Scanner checklist; photos attached to recipes, vehicles, parking spots and birthdays, and the photos ID Photo keeps; logo images you picked for QR codes; letterheads you keep in PDF Sign; GPS Logger tracks; remembered NFC tag passwords; the App Inspector's list of your installed apps; and your Pro entitlement, which Google Play restores on its own. The five encrypted tools keep their own password-protected exports for moving between phones.

The same toggle controls Android's device-to-device transfer, the step that copies your apps when you set up a new phone from this one by cable or Wi-Fi. That copy goes straight from one phone to the other and does not depend on a screen lock. On Android 12 and later it has no size limit and additionally carries the photos, QR logos, PDF Sign letterheads and GPS tracks; on Android 9 to 11 it carries the same set as the cloud backup. Secure Vault, Encrypted Notes, Diary, the Authenticator, Family Papers, PDF Fill drafts, Document Scanner checklist scans, remembered NFC tag passwords, the App Inspector's list and your Pro entitlement are never carried. With the toggle off, Poof's data is not carried there either.

Poof's part of every backup pass always includes one one-byte placeholder file, whether or not the toggle is on. It contains nothing; it is what lets a pass carrying none of your data be a valid, empty backup rather than an error, which would otherwise interrupt the backups of your other apps too. When you turn the toggle off, Poof stops adding your data, and the next backup pass replaces Poof's part of the device backup with that placeholder, so nothing of yours remains there. Android decides when that pass runs, usually overnight while the phone is idle, charging and on Wi-Fi, but it can be much later, and until it runs the previous copy stays. One case needs your help: if you have given Poof notification access for Notification History, Android treats Poof as in use and leaves it out of its automatic backups for as long as that access is on, so neither your latest data nor the change after you turn the toggle off reaches your Google account. Turning off Poof's notifications, or pausing capture inside the tool, does not change this; the notification access itself is what counts. To back up in that case, turn off Poof's notification access in your phone's settings, start a backup from your phone's backup settings (for example, Back up now), and turn the access back on once it has finished. Notifications that arrive while the access is off are not recorded. Setting up a new phone from this one is not affected. To remove the copy immediately, delete this phone's device backup from your Google account settings, which removes that whole backup, not only Poof's part; uninstalling Poof does not remove it. We never see or receive your backups.

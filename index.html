Poof

Privacy Policy

Effective August 31, 2026

This document explains exactly what data Poof accesses on your device, what it does with that data, and what (if anything) leaves your device. Poof is built on three commitments:

  • No ads. Ever.
  • No tracking. No analytics, no crash reporting, no device identifiers, no third-party SDKs.
  • No accounts. You don't sign in. There is no server holding your data because there is no server.

If at any point this policy contradicts what the app actually does, the app is wrong; please contact us so we can fix it.

The short version

Poof is a utility toolkit. Most tools work entirely on your device and never make a network request. Six tools do, and only when you actively use them: Speed Test, Currency Converter, DNS Lookup, URL Unshortener, What's My IP, and Recipes. Four of those contact a fixed service chosen by us. Two, URL Unshortener and Recipes, contact a web address you give them and nothing else. Each is described in full under "What leaves your device" below. No request to any third party contains your contacts, files, location, notifications, or any user-identifying information.

When a tool needs access to something on your device (your camera, microphone, location, contacts, notifications, or installed apps), Android prompts you to grant permission, and you can revoke it at any time from Android Settings. Poof never accesses any of these without an explicit user action.

What we access, and where it goes

1. Location

Tools
Prayer Times, Sun & Moon (sunrise/sunset), Qibla, GPS Logger, Altimeter, Parking Spot Saver.
Accessed
Your device's GPS coordinates (latitude/longitude) via Android's standard Location Manager. Both Approximate and Precise location may be requested; coarse coordinates are used as fallback. GPS Logger additionally records a stream of coordinates over time while you have a recording session active, and may use the Background location permission so the session can continue while the screen is off (you explicitly grant this via Android Settings; the recording works without it while the screen is on and the foreground notification is visible).
Used for
Calculating prayer times, sunrise/sunset, and Qibla bearing locally on the device. Prayer Times stores your last-known coordinates in app-private storage so it can compute prayer times offline. GPS Logger stores the recorded track (a list of points with timestamps and optional altitude/accuracy/speed) in a local database on your device; you can rename, export, or delete tracks at any time. Parking Spot Saver stores the single spot you save, plus any note or photo you attach to it, in parking-spots.db, so it can point you back to the car later.
Goes to
Nowhere. Coordinates never leave your device. There is no Poof server. GPS tracks exported as GPX or KML are saved to a destination you pick via the system share sheet; nothing is uploaded by Poof.

Prayer Times, Sun & Moon, and Qibla accept manual city entry instead of GPS. GPS Logger requires GPS to record. Location permission can be revoked any time from Android Settings.

2. Camera

Tools
QR Generator/Scanner, Document Scanner, Magnifier, Camera Inspector, Image OCR, Live Color Picker, Authenticator (scanning a two-factor setup QR code), Loyalty Cards (scanning the barcode on a physical card), Parking Spot Saver (a photo of where you parked), Vehicle Log (photos of receipts or the odometer).
Accessed
Camera frames in real time.
Used for
On-device processing using Google's ML Kit (the on-device variant: no Firebase ML, no Google Cloud Vision). Frames are analyzed in memory for barcodes, text, document edges, or color.
Goes to
Nowhere off your phone. Live frames are analyzed in memory and never uploaded. The two tools that let you take a photo on purpose, Parking Spot Saver and Vehicle Log, keep that photo in Poof's app-private storage on your device only; deleting the entry deletes the photo.

Goes to
Nowhere. Nothing is transmitted. The Recent Contacts tool clears its in-memory list when closed; Birthdays entries persist on this device until you delete them from the tool.

5. Notifications

Tool
Notification History.
Accessed
When (and only when) you enable Notification History and grant the Notification Listener permission, Poof receives a copy of every notification posted to your device, including the title, body text, and posting app.
Used for
Storing the captured notifications in an on-device database so you can search and review them later.
Goes to
Nowhere we control. Stored locally on your device.

You can:

  • Disable capture entirely from the tool's settings.
  • Exclude specific apps from capture.
  • Skip ongoing/persistent notifications.
  • Suppress text capture (keep titles only).
  • Auto-prune entries older than your chosen retention period.
  • Manually delete individual or all entries.

Notifications can contain sensitive information (one-time codes, message previews, banking alerts). Capture is opt-in and you control what is captured.

7. Files and media you select

Tools
PDF tools (merge, split, compress, watermark, sign, redact, etc.), Image tools (compress, convert, redact, resize, watermark, EXIF viewer, etc.), Video tools (Compressor, Trimmer, Frame Extractor, Video to Audio, Rotate, Speed Changer, Video to GIF, Merger), Audio tools (Trimmer, Speed and Pitch, Merger, Ringtone Maker, Silence Remover), File tools (Archive Tool, Duplicate File Finder, Batch File Renamer, File Splitter / Joiner, Junk Cleaner), OCR, Document Scanner.
Accessed
Only the specific files you pick using Android's system file picker, or shared into Poof via the system share sheet. Poof does not browse your storage.
Used for
Processing on-device using local libraries (PDFBox, Android image APIs, Android Media3 hardware codecs for video and audio, on-device ML Kit, a pure-Java GIF encoder for Video to GIF, Apache Commons Compress for the Archive Tool). Nowhere. No file is uploaded. Temporary working files in the app's cache are deleted when the operation finishes. Long-running operations such as video compression, trimming, audio merging, ringtone creation, GIF encoding, and audio speed changes continue in the background via a foreground service, which posts a progress notification while it works and stops as soon as the queue is done.
System settings
Ringtone Maker offers a "Set as default ringtone / notification / alarm" button after a save. That call requires Android's "Modify system settings" special permission. Poof declares the permission so it can ask Android for it, but Android still requires you to grant it manually in Settings > Apps > Poof > Modify system settings the first time you tap the button. Without the permission, the file still saves and is available through the system pickers; only the one-tap "set as default" affordance is gated. No setting is changed without your explicit tap.

12. Diary

Tool
Diary: private on-device journal with one entry per day, mood tracking, photos, calendar view, streaks, and daily reminders. Every entry is encrypted on this device with AES-256-GCM and a master key generated and held by Android's Keystore. The key requires user authentication (fingerprint, face, or screen-lock credential) before it can be used; once you authenticate, it stays usable for up to ten minutes so you can browse without re-prompting on every tap.
Accessed
The text, mood selection, and photos you choose to attach to each daily entry. Photos are added by picking from your gallery using Android's system Photo Picker (up to 5 per entry). The tool reads your device's wall clock and time zone so the daily reminder fires at the time you configure.
Used for
Storing each entry's content, mood, and photo-path list as ciphertext blobs in an on-device database. Only the date (the primary key) and the created / updated timestamps stay in the clear so the calendar can sort by recency without unlocking. Attached photos are downscaled to roughly 1600 px on the long edge, then encrypted with the same Keystore master key and written to the app's private files directory. The decrypted-in-memory data drives the in-app calendar, streak counter, mood chart, "On this day" memory card, and the optional daily reminder notification. The home-screen widget shows only date-derived information (entry-streak count, "written today" presence flag). it has no auth context and can't decrypt content or mood.
Goes to
Nowhere. Entries and photos live only on this device. There is no cloud copy and no automatic backup. If you uninstall Poof, replace your screen-lock biometric, or use Android's "Clear data", the entries become unrecoverable by design (the Keystore master key is wiped). Sharing exports through Android's standard share sheet routes to whichever app you choose; Poof does not upload anything on its own.
Auto-lock
The diary re-locks the moment the whole app moves to the background. Re-entering the tool prompts for authentication again. The "Lock now" menu option locks manually without leaving. Authentication is handled entirely by Android's secure subsystem; no biometric data is exposed to Poof.
Independent from other tools
Diary uses a separate Keystore alias from Secure Vault and Encrypted Notes. Losing access to one doesn't affect the others; each tool prompts for authentication separately the first time you enter it in a session.
Plain-text exports you can create
The tool offers two unencrypted export formats via Android's system file picker (you choose the destination: local folder, Drive, email attachment, anywhere). These deliberately leave the tool's protection so you can use the data outside Poof:
  • Markdown: one text file containing every entry's date, mood, and content. Photos are not included; the markdown is plain text.
  • PDF: a fully rendered document containing every entry's text and attached photos, paginated. Used for printing.
Importing markdown back into the tool restores the text + mood entries; photo paths are dropped (they reference another device's filesDir and can't be migrated cross-install).
Optional encrypted backup
You can also export the whole diary into a single password-protected .poofdiary file. The format wraps a ZIP (manifest plus one plaintext JPEG per attachment) in AES-256-GCM, with the encryption key derived from your password using PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 over 250 000 iterations. The password is never stored anywhere, transmitted, or recoverable by Poof: if you forget it the backup is unreadable. You choose where the file is saved (local storage, your own cloud, etc.) using Android's system file picker. Restore reads the same format back and re-encrypts photos with the local Keystore key, dedupes by date so re-running the restore doesn't duplicate entries.

The lock gates the screen; it does not separately encrypt the secrets, which are already at rest under the Authenticator's own Keystore key (see above).

15. NFC Tags

16. Secure Vault

Tool
Secure Vault: hide photos behind your phone's screen lock. Imported items are encrypted on-device and a small thumbnail is kept (also encrypted) so the grid can render without re-decrypting the full file each time.
Accessed
The images you pick from your gallery via Android's system Photo Picker. The original files in your gallery are not modified by default. If you turn on "Delete originals after import" in the vault's settings, the original is removed from your gallery once the encrypted copy is safely written; this is an explicit, off-by-default choice that uses Android's standard "delete these items?" system prompt to obtain your consent.
Used for
Storing each item as a ciphertext file under the app's private files directory, encrypted with AES-256-GCM. A random per-item data key encrypts the image bytes; the Keystore master key in turn encrypts the data key. The master key requires user authentication (fingerprint, face, or screen-lock credential) before it can be used; once you authenticate, it stays usable for up to ten minutes so you can browse the vault without re-prompting on every tap. A small encrypted thumbnail is also generated for the grid view. The full image is decrypted into memory only when you tap to view it; nothing plaintext is written back to disk.
Goes to
Nowhere on its own. Vault contents never leave your device. The tool makes no network requests. There is no cloud copy. If you uninstall Poof, replace your screen lock biometric, or use Android's "Clear data" without having exported a password-protected backup (see below), the vault contents are unrecoverable by design. You can also choose to "Export to gallery" individual items or a multi-select batch, in which case the decrypted copy is written back to Pictures/Poof via Android's MediaStore.
Auto-lock
The vault re-locks the moment the whole app moves to the background (recents peek, switching to another app, screen off). Re-entering the tool always prompts for authentication. The lock icon in the top bar locks it manually without leaving the screen. Authentication is handled entirely by Android's secure subsystem; no biometric data is exposed to Poof, and only the master key's metadata is stored.
Optional UI prefs
A "Blur thumbnails" toggle blurs grid previews until you tap an item (purely visual, the underlying ciphertext is unchanged). A sort-order preference (newest first, oldest first, name A to Z or Z to A, size descending or ascending) and the "Delete originals after import" choice are stored in a small unencrypted preferences file. None of this leaks vault contents.
Password-protected backup
You can export the whole vault into a single encrypted file (extension .poofvault) via Android's system file picker, to a destination you choose (local folder, Drive, email attachment, anywhere). The file is encrypted with AES-256-GCM using a key derived from a password you set, via PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 with 250 000 iterations. That password is never stored, and Poof cannot recover it or read the backup without it. Restoring reads such a file back. Poof does not upload or sync anything on its own.
Hide from library
A "Hide from library" toggle in the vault's settings removes Secure Vault from the home grid, library, and search. The tool is still installed and its items are still encrypted at rest; only its discoverability changes. To find it again, long-press the Version row in Settings to open the Hidden Tools manager. No data is exposed or moved by toggling visibility; it is purely a UI-listing preference.

Tool

Nowhere. The tool makes no network requests. There is no cloud copy and no automatic backup. If you uninstall Poof, replace your screen-lock biometric, or use Android's "Clear data", the notes are unrecoverable by design.

Authentication is handled entirely by Android's secure subsystem; no biometric data is exposed to Poof, and only the master key's metadata is stored.
Search
Optional in-tool search filters the already-decrypted list of unlocked notes by title or body, case-insensitive. The search query never leaves your device; no plaintext is written back to disk for indexing.
Optional backup
You can export every note into a single password-protected .poofnotes file using a password you pick. The file contains a JSON document of your notes (title, body, and timestamps) encrypted with AES-256-GCM. The password is never stored anywhere, transmitted, or recoverable by Poof: if you forget it the backup is unreadable. What leaves your device

Six tools, and only when you use them.

Speed Test → Cloudflare

When you run a speed test, the tool exchanges data with Cloudflare's public speed-test infrastructure:

  • https://speed.cloudflare.com/meta : returns your public IP, ISP name, and detected city
  • https://speed.cloudflare.com/__down : downloads test bytes for download-speed measurement
  • https://speed.cloudflare.com/__up : uploads random bytes for upload-speed measurement

Test results (Mbps, latency, jitter) are saved to a local on-device database so you can see your history. Nothing about your test results is sent to Poof or anywhere else. Cloudflare's privacy policy applies to the Cloudflare side: cloudflare.com/privacypolicy

Currency Converter → open.er-api.com

When you open the Currency Converter, it fetches current exchange rates from https://open.er-api.com/v6/latest/{currency}. The request contains only the base-currency code (e.g. USD). Rates are cached on your device so the converter works offline.

DNS Lookup → Cloudflare, Google

When you run a DNS lookup, the tool sends the hostname or IP you typed to one or more public DNS-over-HTTPS resolvers and shows you what they returned. The request contains only the hostname (or IP for reverse lookups) and the record type (A, AAAA, MX, etc.) you picked. The endpoints used are:

  • https://1.1.1.1/dns-query (Cloudflare)
  • https://dns.google/resolve (Google Public DNS)

In Compare mode the same request is sent in parallel to both so you can see how their answers differ. In Single mode only the resolver you chose is contacted. The tool can also resolve via the device's system DNS for A and AAAA records (your phone's normal resolver path, controlled by Android Settings or Private DNS), in which case the request goes wherever Android sends it. Each resolver's privacy policy applies to its side: Cloudflare, Google Public DNS.

Recipes → whichever recipe page you link to

When you paste a recipe link into Recipes, or share one to Poof from your browser, the tool fetches that page and looks for the structured recipe data (schema.org Recipe, published as JSON-LD) that most recipe sites already include for search engines. Like the URL Unshortener, and unlike the other tools here, it does not contact a fixed service: it contacts the address you gave it, and nothing else. There is no intermediary, no proxy, and no server of ours involved at any point.

The request is a plain GET from your device's normal IP, with a generic Poof user-agent string and no cookies, no identifiers, and nothing about you or your cookbook attached. The site can see your IP making the request, in the same way it would if you opened the page in a browser. The response is read up to a two megabyte limit and then discarded; only the recipe fields you approve on the review screen are kept, and only once you press Save.

If the page carries a photo and you leave the photo switch on, one further request is made to that same site for that image, which is then downscaled and stored on your device. Turning the switch off means no image request is made at all. No other address is ever contacted, no matter what the page contains.

URL Unshortener → whichever URL you paste

The URL Unshortener follows every redirect of a short URL you give it and shows the final destination plus a preview of that page's title and description. This is different from the tools above: it doesn't contact a fixed service. It contacts whatever URL you typed and whatever URL that one redirects to, all the way to the end of the chain (up to ten hops). Each request goes out from your device's normal IP, with a generic browser-style User-Agent and no cookies or identifiers.

By design, this means the destination server can see your IP making the request, in the same way it would if you tapped the link in any browser. That is the point of the tool: it visits the URL on your behalf so you can see where it goes before deciding to open it yourself. Nothing about the traced URL or the destination is stored after you leave the screen.

What's My IP → ipwho.is, icanhazip.com

When you open the What's My IP tool, it makes up to three requests, all in parallel:

  • https://ipwho.is/ : returns your public IP plus geolocation and network metadata (city, region, country, ISP, ASN, timezone). Whichever IP version your device's preferred stack chose comes back here.
  • https://ipv4.icanhazip.com : returns your IPv4 address as plain text, or fails silently if your network has no IPv4 path.
  • https://ipv6.icanhazip.com : returns your IPv6 address as plain text, or fails silently if your network has no IPv6 path.

Each service identifies you by the source IP of the request itself, the same way any website sees your IP. No request body, no API key, no account, no device identifier. The tool does not store the result; closing the screen discards it. icanhazip.com is operated by Cloudflare. The relevant privacy policies are: ipwho.is, Cloudflare.

No personally identifying information is sent to any of these services. All requests are made from your device's IP (same as any web browser) and contain no Poof-assigned user ID or device identifier (because none exists).
